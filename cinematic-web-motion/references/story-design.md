# Cinematic Story Design

## Goal

Design a visual story before writing animation code.

A cinematic web sequence should answer:

- What is happening?
- Why does it matter?
- What changes?
- What does the brand do about it?
- What should the visitor do next?

## Scene template

For each scene define:

- purpose
- subject
- camera position
- camera movement
- scroll range
- visual state
- overlay content
- transition into next scene

Example:

| Scene | Purpose | Motion |
|---|---|---|
| Establish | Introduce situation | Slow tracking |
| Disruption | Create tension | Deceleration |
| Freeze | Create attention | Motion stops |
| Reveal | Introduce solution | Camera movement |
| Scale | Show reach | Pullback |
| Resolution | Show result | Motion resumes |
| CTA | Convert | Controlled settle |

## Story rule

The visitor should be able to understand the story even without reading every word.

Motion should communicate:

- scale
- speed
- connection
- transformation
- problem
- resolution

## Recurring visual anchor

Use one object as a visual anchor when possible.

Examples:

- vehicle
- product
- building
- person
- package
- device

The anchor can move through different scales or environments.

This creates continuity.

## Avoid

Do not use:

- arbitrary camera movement
- random 3D objects
- visual effects without meaning
- rapid cuts for scroll-controlled sequences
- text walls inside the cinematic scene

## Example: Mobility in Standstill

A roadside-assistance company:

1. Vehicle travels through a realistic German landscape.
2. The vehicle begins to slow.
3. Vehicle stops.
4. Rain, road and environment freeze.
5. The solution enters the narrative.
6. Camera pulls far away.
7. Germany becomes visible.
8. A coordinated network is revealed.
9. Camera returns to the vehicle.
10. Assistance arrives.
11. Vehicle continues.
12. Website content and CTA take over.

The visual message is:

"One problem at one location can be coordinated through a nationwide system."
